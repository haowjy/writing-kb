# Battle / Action Scene Technique

Load for battles, chases, attacks, and physical confrontations. Movement and
positioning are the physical foundation; the emphasis depends on what this
particular fight should make the reader experience. Do not give every battle
the same proportions of tactics, spectacle, emotion, and speech.

## Choose the focus, and let it change

Before drafting or cutting, identify the combatants' objectives, the POV's role
(participant, commander, spectator, or endangered bystander), and the dominant
reader experience. Winning a match, surviving an assault, protecting someone,
and forcing an opponent to acknowledge a claim are different objectives.

| Component | What earns attention |
| --- | --- |
| Movement and positioning | Closing distance, retreating, gaining an angle, losing sight, finding cover, blocking escape, and exploiting or surviving an opening. |
| Power and atmosphere | How the encounter looks, sounds, and feels: accumulating charge, heat, light, impact, scale, damage, and the environment's response. |
| Emotion and relationship | Confidence, frustration, fear, exhilaration, desperation, trust, and grief as advantage changes or someone important is threatened. |
| Speech | Commands, warnings, encouragement, pleas, challenges, and arguments that express what the participants are fighting for or against. |
| Perception and time | What can be seen or inferred, what remains uncertain, how long an attack takes, and the suspense or recovery between exchanges. |

These components interact, rather than taking turns by quota. A competitive
fight can foreground technique and excitement; a personal confrontation can
foreground speech and emotion without losing physical continuity. A battle
may change emphasis midway through.

In Chapter 15, Electabuzz blocks access to the children while Amber seeks an
exit. Its apparent defeat shifts attention to Ash calling and Amber listening;
its return briefly restores hope. The execution and Fuji's demands make
helplessness and his claim over the family central. This is the focus of this
battle, not a template for every encounter. [ch15]

## Render the movement, not just the move names

Track where opponents, trainers, bystanders, cover, and exits are relative to
the current exchange. Make significant approaches, retreats, obstructions,
and openings legible. Small directional words can matter: stepping back from
a charge is more specific than stepping to the edge.

A command is not the attack itself. When the encounter depends on launch,
interception, buildup, or impact, let the reader experience those stages. In
Chapter 15, Magmar sends fire across the room before Electabuzz's Thunderbolt
tears through it; the counter does not substitute for showing the incoming
attack. [ch15] Routine exchanges can still be compressed. Do not turn this
into a mandatory checklist for every move.

Keep species-specific motion and mechanics:

> Electricity coursed around his fists as he whirled them like a windmill. [ch15]

Here the comparison specifies the movement. The repeated rotations build
charge and establish Electabuzz's fighting technique; they are not disposable
merely because they recur. Vary the attention as the charge builds, releases,
or becomes harder to sustain, rather than renaming the same motion each time.

The wording also carries a recognition reward: readers unfamiliar with the
lore can picture the technique, while familiar readers can enjoy seeing it
realized in combat. Do not add a Pokedex explanation just to make everyone
catch the connection. This is part of the broader preference for
[word connections and allusions](tone.md#word-connections-and-allusions).

## Stay within the chosen perception

Legible action does not require omniscient sight. Smoke, fire, distance, injury,
and divided attention can hide an exchange. Give the available evidence and
let uncertainty persist until the viewpoint can learn more.

> Smoke billowed from Magmar, hiding both Pokemon. A thud came from inside the cloud, followed by a sharp hiss. White-blue light flickered through the smoke. [ch15]

Magmar staggering out supplies the visible aftermath. Do not identify the exact
uppercut or contact point through the smoke, or make a successful screen fail
just to keep the choreography visible.

Narration follows perception, not necessarily the order in which causes become
visible. Amber feels the earthquake before looking back toward its source.
The rumble prompts attention; the obscured purple figure stays unidentified.
Do not reverse those beats to force a cause-first presentation. [ch15]

Chapters with a panoramic register can show more of the field; do not impose
Amber's restricted first-person access on every battle.

## Give attacks and pauses their duration

One exchange can occupy several sentences. An electrical discharge and Magmar
staggering under it deserve separate beats in Chapter 15; combining both into
one clause chain made the action feel abbreviated. Conversely, an attack and
its response can share a paragraph when they form one continuous movement.
Neither one-move-per-line nor one-exchange-per-sentence is a rule.

Place orientation where attention naturally shifts. Establishing the kitchen
viewpoint during Amber's crawl preserves geography without interrupting
Electabuzz's lunge just before Fuji answers it. Read the lead-in and the next
paragraph after every local edit.

Let waiting and recovery actually take time. Ash's unanswered calls suspend
the fight around Electabuzz's fate. After the earthquake, Amber stays down as
the ground settles and takes a few breaths before looking. Compressing eyes
shut, ground settling, and getting up into an instantaneous action list loses
the experience of surviving it. [ch15]

## Preserve atmosphere and emotional participation

> Magmar's flames cast writhing shadows across the walls as it stepped through the ruined doorframe, the floorboards smoking beneath its feet. [ch15]

This gives the room changing light, heat, and a threatening presence. It is
not surplus because the reader already knows Magmar is dangerous. Likewise,
metal crumpling and hissing around Magmar makes the collision and heat felt.
Sensory pleasure and scale need not introduce a new tactic to earn space.

Reduce inflated or stacked comparisons selectively; do not equate all imagery
with excess. The elaborate needles comparison for static can go while the
shadows and windmill motion stay. Prefer wording that gives a clear picture:
stray bolts striking rubble is more immediately legible than electricity
"earthing" there.

Let emotional reactions develop with the fight. A participant gaining ground,
a trainer watching a partner struggle, and a frightened bystander need not
respond alike. Pokemon also react and persist; they are not just move-delivery
systems. Respect the POV's access to their feelings.

Heartbeats, trembling, urgent delivery, and direct emotional impressions are
available tools, not banned phrases. Repetition is a problem when it repeats
the same emotional beat without developing it. Fear can begin, recede with
hope, and intensify again. Do not prescribe a symptom after every hit or cut
all reactions to avoid repetition. Amber can also retain her own wit under
pressure without being required to make the whole battle funny; see
[Amber narration](char-amber-narration.md#rhythm-and-humor).

## Let speech participate in the fight

Speech can carry the struggle's personal or ideological meaning as well as
its tactics. Fuji's claim that he is here for his family, Amber's warning to
Electabuzz, and Ash's plea for his father's partner do different work. Fuji
later treats Amber's tactical help as disobedience: an intervention in the
battle becomes part of their spoken confrontation. [ch15]

Do not strip these exchanges down to commands, or insert banter mechanically
between moves. Fit speech to distance, noise, breath, available time, and who
currently controls the encounter. A pause, standoff, withdrawal, or defeated
opponent can support a longer exchange; active opponents should not silently
freeze so a speech can finish. Let wording, delivery, and interruptions show
confidence or loss of control when those changes matter.

Amber's game thought remains excited, biased, uncertain, or wrong when the
scene calls for it. Her commands need not be optimal:

> "Ditto, dodge and transform!" [ch4]

The command confuses Ditto. Her battle understanding develops through the
encounter rather than arriving as expert knowledge. [ch4]

## Revision gate

Compare with the baseline, not just the last shortened draft. For each cut,
ask what is lost: movement, anticipation, viewpoint access, atmosphere,
emotion, speech, duration, or voice. Preserving the winner and the move order
is not enough. Fix genuine staging problems in the baseline rather than
restoring them blindly. See [battle revision risks](../issues/battle-scale.md).

Craft comments preserve non-obvious intent, mechanical constraints, or a
knowledge boundary worth protecting. They should not paraphrase an obvious
effect or narrate what was deleted. Read the compiled prose without them.
